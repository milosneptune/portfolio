A couple thoughts regarding abstraction in *[Room for Growth's]*(/portfolio/projects/rfg/) design

# What is Abstraction?
Abstraction is defined as a separation, or a pulling away from concrete physical reality, and into a theoretical lens. For video games, abstractions and jumps in logic can be found all over the place. They are considered the "mental leaps and bridges" players cross when connecting reality to the virtuality or the suspension of disbelief one engages with to enjoy a fictional piece of media to its fullest potential.

Jesper Juul states that: ["...abstraction as something that the player decodes while playing a game, and abstraction as a type of optimization that the player builds over time”](https://www.jesperjuul.net/text/acertainlevel/), meaning that the player first interprets what the various levels of abstraction *really* convey, and then *optimizes* it by examining that abstraction, and learning the strategy of the genre. 

I mean, in most role-playing games involving directional movement, the player understands that a keypress of W translates to movement in the virtual space, of a virtual character they *control* and even will refer to that character as *themself* in some cases. This is a mental leap that the player has to make in order to enjoy the game to its fullest. 

You may be asking, well, what exactly does abstraction do for the player? To that, I answer, many! To name a few, abstraction provides these benefits:
- Eases cognitive load by managing the conceptual space the player experiences
- Cause and effect become easily identifiable 
- Removes extraneous factors, as real life is messy and complicated
- Reduces time required to grasp the concepts

So, we've established that abstraction is a necessary part of video game design and is essential to curating the player experience. Now, I'd like to explore how abstraction is an important principle I've weighed in the design for the Resonant Voices game, *Room for Growth*.
# Abstraction in *Room For Growth*
*Room for Growth*--like many games--abstracts a number of ideas, ranging from small "flavor" details, to the larger theme injected within. The intentions behind *Room for Growth* is to "simulate" a system where you are designed to fail, seeking to capture the frustrations that may be felt when dealing with real-life systems. As one can imagine, this particular theme involves various degrees of abstraction
### the largest lens
The first layer of abstraction *RFG* faces, the major crux, boils down to abstracting real-life oppressive systems. Picking one system to base the game around would lean too heavily into the educational games realm, so ultimately, we attempted to abstract various systems by picking one quality the majority of them share: a feeling of frustration towards the system. This specific decision is influenced by the benefits of abstraction mentioned above: we can ease cognitive load and have the player discern the intent in a more manageable sense and highlight a "cause and effect" relationship. In the end, the setting chosen was to have the player take care of a plant that would eventually end up dying over a progression of a few days. 

While the design of *RFG* recently went through a large pivot (see Nicholas Binetti's *[The Power of Pivoting](https://www.echoes-vip.org/blog/posts/20261028)*), the original concept I pitched in the Fall of 2025 designated that the frustration would be caused by the ever-changing in-game interactions between the mechanics. This inconsistent relationship between the player and the game was another degree of abstraction; the player would interpret the game as a system (well, it is, but in a less technical sense) more akin to the institutions that affect us everyday, and themself as the targeted minority group of this system. 
### control schemes
*Room for Growth*'s original controls--before the grand pivot--involved *only* using the mouse to perform all the game actions, and seldom used the keyboard. This decision was made for a variety of reasons, namely the ease of onboarding, but raised many questions in my head. Would constricting the control of the player to only exist as a cursor aid in the frustration? Would the player see themselves as the cursor, migrating across the screen? How is this control schema going to affect the emotional arc versus one that had utilized the keyboard? 
### plant vs. player
Earlier, I had mentioned that the preceding *RFG* design placed a separation between the player, and the plant. One integral piece of our design pivot was the removal of this difference. In a way, we removed another layer of abstraction, but abstracting also means to remove, so you could call this an abstraction of an abstraction...?. By merging the two, I believed it would be easier to convey that feeling of frustration we were looking for, since it would also eliminate excess design questions such as is the system targeting the player as its subject, or the plant? By having the player *be* the plant, rather than some abstracted entity, we became able to peel back a layer. With the player and the plant merging, the actions the player can take were also re-evaluated and transformed into two options: growing up or growing downwards. This is another abstraction of real life, no plant has to take "turns" in growing, or can only pick one or the other during their turn (at least, to my knowledge). 
### reductions
The setting, the background, the aesthetic produce the spatial understanding of *Room for Growth*. This perception of space also comes from an abstraction of reality. Reality becomes reduced, or abstracted to encompass this limited space, and the player understands that this limited space is chosen purposefully and carefully. As I said earlier, we minimize the complexity by only having the player interact with a specific type of setting--which is a greenhouse. 

On a similar note of "taking away" from its design, we take a careful approach to the *explicit* showing of humans to avoid assumptions of what specific system this is abstracting. Alternatively, the graphic inclusion of a human may cause the player to blame that fictional individual, rather than the game. Interestingly enough, during concept art showings at playtests, a couple viewers mentioned gardeners or were under the impression they would be taken care of by some other entity. As *RFG* continues development, I wonder if this issue will re-rise and how we will attempt to moderate this assumption. 
# Final Challenges
 *Room for Growth* has served as an incredible design challenge. I suppose that the final question that we are sprinting to meet, is will the player understand the abstraction of systematic oppression? Will they experience the frustrations we have intended? Does our abstracted layers muddle the theme or not? I think I rack through my brain about these questions on the daily basis and often stump myself into walls trying to figure out the best possible option, but really the only way to find out for sure is to continue development and keep playtesting.
